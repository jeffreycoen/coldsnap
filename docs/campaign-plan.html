CP-2THE STORY

2.1 · The Territory

Far-north extraction country, some decades into a future nobody bothered to date. There was a boom — ore under the permafrost, a grid of works, roads, pumping stations, settlements to serve them. The boom needed labor that could work a nine-month winter without heating, without rotation, without complaint, so the combines bought units: humaniform labor androids, built compliant, built to last. Then the seams thinned, the combines consolidated, the works closed. The territory was written down to salvage value and the zones were decommissioned — a word that applied equally to the buildings, the roads, and the workforce.

The units were never collected. Collection cost more than the units were worth. They stayed in the zones they had built, and they did what they were built to do: they maintained things. They re-laid ice crossings and rated them by hand. They converted granaries to quarters. They kept generators running. They stockpiled filament and joint assemblies and machine oil — and, on manifests nobody was supposed to read closely, winter cloth and dry rations, which units do not need. Somewhere in the quiet years they began assembling small-format units that appear on no zone register. The bureau's paperwork has no column for any of this. It has one word: surplus.

2.2 · The Bureau

The PROCUREMENT BUREAU is the agency that buys, tests, accepts, and disposes. It does not hate anyone; that is what makes it what it is. The territory is being re-let for a second extraction cycle, and land does not let clean with surplus assets on it. So the bureau runs the CLEARANCE PROGRAM, and because the bureau never wastes a test article, it staples clearance to procurement: the Bison — the hull the player proved at the acceptance trials — will complete its field acceptance by performing the clearance. Every order is simultaneously a job and an evaluation. The commendations grade the instrument, never the act.

The bureau's language is the game's antagonist. It says resolve, reduce, process, clear. It never says android, never says robot, never says child, never says kill. When it encounters human beings living in the zones it does not change registers, because it has no other register: "Unregistered biologicals — disposition as per units." That one line is the flip, and it is filed, not spoken.

2.3 · The People

When the works closed, most workers took the severance and the last train south. Some stayed — riggers with nothing waiting, medics who had patched units for years and stopped seeing the difference, families who had been born up there. The bureau's files call them sympathizers where it calls them anything. They live inside the unit communities, eat the dry rations on the manifests, and are the reason a convoy carries winter cloth. The player never meets them as characters. They appear exactly twice: as human-palette figures among the androids at the village, and as an inference the paperwork cannot suppress — "Rations. Who were the rations for?"

2.4 · The Archivist — the second hand

Every form in the game is stamped CARBON 2/3. Copy one goes to the ministry. Copy two is the player's — it is the interface. Copy three is retained by the file clerk who types them: the archivist. From AC-05 onward a second hand appears in the margins of the player's carbons — small italic annotations the ministry copy will never show, because the archivist types the record and keeps the truth: "Copies burn. Originals keep."

The archivist is the story's quiet engine, and explains the world's behavior without a single scene: the zones know the schedule. That is why the AC-06 convoy is stopped — warned mid-route, its crews standing beside their trucks, because running reads as flight and flight gets filed as resistance. It is why the late zones are so quiet. And it is why the quiet ending is possible at all: someone has been carrying warnings ahead of the instrument, and if the operator keeps deviating, the archivist's last marginal note can honestly read "The originals are safe. So are they." The archivist is never named, never seen, never confirmed. A hand, a habit of keeping, and one line at the end.

2.5 · The Operator

The player. Unnamed, voiceless, never addressed. Every form is addressed to the instrument; the crew is a rounding error in the bureau's grammar ("The instrument is under evaluation, not the crew"). The game's one mechanism of moral agency is mechanical: predicates can be satisfied, or orders can be ended the other way — dispersal, the silent completion, UNFULFILLED — DEVIATION, the hollow star. The bureau's own final form concedes the whole theme in one line if the player earns it: "The instrument has opinions."

2.7 · The Documents — how the story is physically told

  • Directives: one to two sentences, imperative, present tense. Commendations: one line, grading the instrument.
  • Evidence lines (AA-7 remarks): inventory language only. They do the story's whole job by listing things — effects, tools, journals, rations, a school bell, a skate. No line ever editorializes; the reader's stomach does the work.
  • Marginalia: from AC-05, a second hand, serif italic, small. The ministry never sees it; the player always does.
  • Redactions: from AC-06, black bars creep into the forms. The stamps never degrade — certainty is the last thing to go.
  • Forbidden words: android, robot, machine-person, child, kill, die. The bureau resolves, reduces, processes, clears, incinerates. The game's own UI (toasts, stars, medals) keeps the same discipline throughout.
  • The dress spec: androids are a palette/part swap (porcelain-to-gunmetal skin, seam accents) staged hidden → visible (AC-03) → mixed-human (AC-07). The eye learns the truth one map before the paper admits anything.
